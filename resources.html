1. Study habits that actually work

Studying for hours does not always mean learning effectively. The way you study can make a big difference in how much you remember.

Use active recall

Instead of reading the same page repeatedly, close your notes and try to explain what you have learned from memory. Check your notes afterward to identify what you missed.

Example: After learning five new words, write down their meanings without looking at your notes.

Space out your learning

Review information over several days rather than trying to learn everything in one sitting. You could review a topic today, tomorrow, three days later and again the following week.

Break large tasks into smaller ones

Instead of setting a goal to study English for three hours, start with a specific task, such as learning ten words or completing one grammar exercise.

Test yourself regularly

Practice questions help you discover what you understand and what needs more attention.

2. English grammar quick reference

These simple grammar rules can help you avoid common errors in everyday speaking and writing.

For and since

Use for to describe a period of time. Use since to identify when something began.

  • Correct: I have studied English for three years.
  • Correct: I have studied English since 2023.

Fewer and less

Use fewer with things you can count individually. Use less with uncountable amounts.

  • Correct: There are fewer students today.
  • Correct: I have less time this afternoon.

Subject–verb agreement

In the simple present tense, singular third-person subjects usually take a verb ending in -s or -es.

  • Correct: She works every morning.
  • Incorrect: She work every morning.
  • Correct: They work every morning.

Common prepositions

  • At 7 o'clock.
  • On Monday.
  • In October.
  • Interested in learning.
  • Good at mathematics.

Some prepositions depend on the expression being used, so learning them in complete phrases is often helpful.

3. Common English mistakes and their corrections

Small grammatical errors can affect how clearly you communicate. Learn these common corrections and practice using them.

Incorrect: She don't like rice.

Correct: She doesn't like rice.

Use doesn't with he, she and it in the simple present negative. Use don't with I, you, we and they.

Incorrect: I am having 21 years.

Correct: I am 21 years old.

In English, we normally use the verb be to describe someone's age.

Incorrect: I discussed about the problem.

Correct: I discussed the problem.

The verb discuss takes a direct object. In standard usage, you do not need to add about.

Incorrect: He is more taller than me.

Correct: He is taller than me.

Do not normally combine more with an adjective that already uses the comparative ending -er.

Incorrect: I look forward to meet you.

Correct: I look forward to meeting you.

In the expression look forward to, the word to is a preposition. Follow it with a noun or the -ing form of a verb.

Incorrect: One of my friend is here.

Correct: One of my friends is here.

After one of, use a plural noun because you are referring to one member of a group.

4. Commonly confused English words

Some English words sound alike or look similar but have different meanings. Understanding the difference helps you write more accurately.

Words Difference Example
Affect / Effect Affect is usually a verb; effect is usually a noun. The weather affects my mood. The effect was noticeable.
Its / It's Its shows possession; it's means it is or it has. The dog wagged its tail. It's a friendly dog.
Your / You're Your shows possession; you're means you are. Your book is here. You're welcome.
There / Their / They're There refers to a place or introduces a statement; their shows possession; they're means they are. Put it there. Their house is large. They're ready.
Then / Than Then relates to time or sequence; than is used for comparisons. We ate, then left. She is taller than I am.
Accept / Except Accept means to receive or agree; except means excluding. I accept your offer. Everyone came except John.
Lose / Loose Lose means to misplace or fail to win; loose means not tight. Don't lose your keys. This shirt is loose.
Advice / Advise Advice is a noun; advise is a verb. Thank you for your advice. I advise you to practice.
Principal / Principle Principal can mean a school leader or something most important; principle means a rule or belief. The principal spoke. Honesty is an important principle.
Rise / Raise Rise usually happens without a direct object; raise usually acts on something. Prices rise. They raise prices.

Learning tip: Write your own sentence for each pair of words. Creating examples helps you remember how to use them.

5. How to build a stronger English vocabulary

A strong vocabulary helps you express your thoughts more clearly, understand what you read and communicate with greater confidence.

Learn words in context

Do not memorize a word's meaning alone. Learn how it appears in a sentence.

Example: The word reluctant means unwilling or hesitant to do something.

Example sentence: She was reluctant to speak in front of the audience.

Keep a vocabulary notebook

For each new word, record:

  • The word and its meaning.
  • One example sentence.
  • A synonym or related word, when useful.
  • The date you learned it.

Learn word families

Related words can help you understand how English vocabulary works.

  • Decide — decision — decisive.
  • Educate — education — educational.
  • Communicate — communication — communicative.
  • Create — creation — creative.

Notice that related words can have different grammatical roles. For example, create is a verb, while creation is a noun.

Use new words regularly

Choose three new words each day and use them in your own sentences. Review them later in the week.

Read a little every day

Reading articles, short stories and educational materials exposes you to vocabulary in natural contexts. When you encounter an unfamiliar word, use the surrounding sentence to make an initial guess, then check a reliable dictionary.

Practice vocabulary and see how well you understand different words.

6. How to improve your typing speed and accuracy

Typing faster is useful, but accuracy should come first. Developing good habits can help you type more comfortably and efficiently.

Learn the home-row position

On a standard QWERTY keyboard, place the fingers of your left hand on A, S, D and F, and the fingers of your right hand on J, K, L and the semicolon key. Keep your thumbs near the spacebar.

Focus on accuracy before speed

If you repeatedly make mistakes while trying to type faster, slow down slightly. Build accuracy first, then gradually increase your pace.

Practice consistently

Even five to ten minutes of focused practice can be useful when done regularly. Try to practice a little each day instead of relying on occasional long sessions.

Use a comfortable posture

  • Sit in a comfortable, supported position.
  • Keep your shoulders relaxed.
  • Position your screen at a comfortable viewing height.
  • Avoid pressing the keys harder than necessary.

Understand your typing results

WPM means words per minute. It measures typing speed. Accuracy measures how much of your typing is correct.

A higher WPM is useful, but a fast result with many errors may not be as practical as a slightly slower, more accurate result.

7. A simple 15-minute daily English routine

You do not need to study everything at once. A short, consistent routine can help you build your skills over time.

Minutes 1–5: Vocabulary

Learn three new words, check their meanings and write a sentence using each one.

Minutes 6–10: Grammar

Review one grammar rule and answer a few questions. Focus on understanding why an answer is correct.

Minutes 11–15: Practice

Write a short paragraph, read a passage aloud or complete a typing exercise. Choose the activity that best supports your current learning goal.

Review your progress

At the end of the week, revisit the words and grammar rules you learned. Notice what has become easier and what still needs practice.

Remember: Consistency matters more than trying to master everything in one day.

8. How to write clearer English sentences

Clear writing helps readers understand your message without unnecessary effort. Start with simple, complete sentences and add details when they are useful.

Make sure your sentence has a clear subject and verb

A subject tells us who or what the sentence is about. A verb tells us what the subject does or what state it is in.

  • Incomplete: Because I was tired.
  • Complete: I went to bed early because I was tired.

Avoid unnecessarily long sentences

If a sentence contains too many ideas, consider dividing it into two sentences.

  • Less clear: I woke up late and missed the bus and I arrived at school late because I had not prepared my things the previous night.
  • Clearer: I woke up late and missed the bus. I arrived at school late because I had not prepared my things the previous night.

Check punctuation

Use a period to end a statement, a question mark to end a direct question and commas to separate items in a list or help organize a sentence where appropriate.

Proofread your work

Before submitting an assignment or sending an important message, check spelling, punctuation, grammar and whether your meaning is clear.

Reading your writing aloud can help you notice awkward sentences and missing words.

Practice grammar to strengthen the basics of clear writing.
